POLS Courses

Political science explores how power, governance, and political ideas shape societies and influence our world. At the University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa, students examine politics from local and global perspectives, exploring democratic institutions, international relations, political theory, law, and public policy. The curriculum brings particular depth to the study of Hawaiʻi, the Pacific, and Indigenous political thought, connecting these perspectives with broader questions of justice, sovereignty, conflict, and social change. From introductory courses to advanced research, students develop the knowledge and analytical skills to understand political challenges, evaluate competing ideas, and consider possibilities for the future.

Lower-Division Courses (100–299)

Lower-division courses introduce the fundamental ideas, institutions, and processes of political life. Students explore American and international politics, government and public policy, globalization, and the relationships between politics, media, and society. Courses also introduce Indigenous and Native Hawaiian perspectives, the political history of Hawaiʻi, and questions of race, inequality, war, and peace. Through critical reading, writing, and discussion, students begin developing the skills to evaluate political arguments, examine different perspectives, and understand how political decisions affect communities around the world.

{% endcall %} {% call imageTextRow('/media/units/pols/courses/course-02.webp') %}

Upper-Division Courses (300–499)

Upper-division courses offer opportunities to investigate political issues in greater depth and from a wider range of theoretical, historical, and cultural perspectives. Students explore international relations, comparative politics, political philosophy, constitutional law, public policy, and the institutions that shape political life. Specialized studies examine Indigenous sovereignty, Native Hawaiian politics, environmental governance, gender and justice, political movements, and the changing relationships between states and societies. Courses also encourage students to connect theory with practice through policy analysis, independent research, community engagement, internships, and other applied learning experiences.

{% endcall %} {% call imageTextRow('/media/units/pols/courses/course-03.webp') %}

Graduate Courses (600–699)

Graduate courses provide advanced study of political ideas, institutions, and processes while developing the theoretical and methodological foundations for scholarly research. Students engage with political philosophy, international relations, comparative politics, public administration, law, and public policy, alongside specialized studies of Indigenous politics, Hawaiian political thought, and the Asia-Pacific region. Emphasis is placed on critical inquiry, research design, analytical methods, and the evaluation of competing political explanations. Students develop the intellectual tools to investigate complex political questions and contribute to ongoing scholarly conversations.

{% endcall %} {% call imageTextRow('/media/units/pols/courses/course-04.webp') %}

Advanced Graduate Study (700–800)

Advanced graduate study emphasizes specialized scholarship, independent inquiry, and original research. Through research seminars and sustained investigation, students examine complex questions in political theory, international relations, comparative politics, public administration, and public policy. Areas of inquiry also include Indigenous sovereignty, decolonial futures, political development, and alternative approaches to governance and social change. Advanced research methods, scholarly writing, and thesis or dissertation work provide opportunities to deepen expertise, challenge established perspectives, and contribute new knowledge to the discipline.

{% endcall %}
